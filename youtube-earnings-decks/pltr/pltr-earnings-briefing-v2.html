02 / How It Works
02 · How It Works

Palantir doesn't sell an app. It sells a map of your reality.

Most organizations' data is scattered — spreadsheets, sensors, supply-chain systems, satellite feeds — that don't talk to each other. Palantir's core idea, the ontology, links all of it into one digital model of an organization: its people, assets, events, and decisions, connected like a living graph. Analysts, operators, and now AI agents work off that single model instead of hunting across a dozen disconnected systems.

03 / Object Archetypes
03 · The Ontology's Building Blocks

Every customer's ontology is built from the same six archetypes.

A hospital, a bank, and a battlefield look nothing alike — but under the hood, Palantir models all of them with the same small set of reusable object archetypes, customized with each customer's own fields and relationships. The archetypes don't change; what they represent does.

Person

Who's involved

Individuals in the system: employees, patients, suspects, customers, operators — anyone the model needs to track or reason about.

Organization

Who they belong to

Companies, agencies, business units, and teams — the structures people and assets sit inside.

Asset

What gets tracked

Equipment, vehicles, inventory, and infrastructure — the physical or digital things being managed or moved.

Event

What happened

Transactions, incidents, sensor readings, and actions — anything that occurs at a point in time and changes the state of the model.

Location

Where it happened

Facilities, geospatial points, and regions — the "where" that ties people, assets, and events to real-world geography.

Document

What was written down

Contracts, reports, and other unstructured records — the paper trail linked back into the structured model.

Actions and links connect these archetypes into a live graph — so "this person," "this asset," and "this event" are the same underlying objects an analyst, an operator, and an AI agent all reason about together.

04 / Product Stack
04 · The Product Stack

Four platforms, one underlying idea.

Gotham

The original

Built for defense and intelligence. Fuses satellite, sensor, and field data in real time so analysts can see a battlefield or a threat as one picture instead of a dozen feeds.

Used by: militaries, intelligence agencies
Foundry

The commercial sibling

Connects a company's ERP, supply chain, IoT, and financial data through 200+ prebuilt connectors, so every team works off the same live picture.

Used by: airlines, automakers, hospitals, banks
AIP

The AI layer

Plugs large language models into an organization's own ontology, so AI can reason about real operations — without the underlying data ever leaving the customer's control.

Launched 2023 · today's growth engine
Apollo

The delivery engine

Continuously and securely ships software into any environment — cloud, on-prem, classified, even disconnected edge sites — with no manual deployment.

The plumbing that makes the rest work anywhere

06 / AIP Bootcamps
06 · AIP Deep Dive

The real growth engine isn't a feature. It's a sales motion.

Most enterprise software gets sold with a slide deck and a multi-quarter pilot. Palantir sells AIP with a bootcamp: a short, intensive, hands-on build session where Palantir engineers sit with a prospect's own data and ship a working application before the week is out — production use cases, not sandboxed demo data.

Day 1–2 · Scope & connectPalantir engineers work with the prospect's own data, wired through Foundry, without a lengthy procurement cycle first.
Day 3–4 · Build liveReal use cases get built and iterated on in the room, in front of the business users who'll actually use them.
Day 5 · Ship something realThe bootcamp ends with a working application in production, not a proposal — the "pitch" is the delivered product.
Why it compresses the sales cycleTraditional enterprise software can take 12–18 months from first call to signed contract. Bootcamps let prospects feel value in days, then expand from a live deployment instead of a slide deck.
Why It Matters For The Model

Management points to the bootcamp motion as a key driver behind U.S. commercial revenue's acceleration to 133% YoY growth — it's as much a distribution advantage as a product one.

07 / AIP Archetypes
07 · The Shapes AIP Agents Take

Not one AI agent. Four recurring roles.

Every AIP deployment reasons over the same ontology objects introduced earlier — but the agents built on top of them fall into a small set of recurring roles, distinguished mainly by how much autonomy they're given.

Copilot

Assists, doesn't act alone

Sits alongside a human decision-maker, surfacing context and recommended next steps in real time. The human stays in the loop and pulls the trigger.

Autonomous Operator

Acts within guardrails

Executes routine, well-defined actions on its own — rerouting a shipment, flagging a transaction — inside limits the customer sets up front.

Monitor

Watches and raises alerts

Continuously scans a live data stream and surfaces anomalies as they happen, without taking action itself.

Orchestrator

Coordinates the other three

Chains multiple agents and systems together across a multi-step workflow, handing off between them as a process runs end to end.

The archetype a customer starts with tends to track how much they trust the model with their data and their decisions — most deployments begin as a Copilot and only earn autonomy over time.

14 / The "Skynet" Narrative
14 · The "Skynet" Narrative

The bear case Wall Street doesn't put in a model: reputation risk.

Gotham — the platform that built Palantir's reputation — was designed for defense and intelligence work, and critics have never let that go. The company is a recurring target of activists, journalists, and some of its own employees over its military and government surveillance contracts, and detractors have nicknamed it a "Skynet" stock, after the fictional AI that turns on humanity in Terminator. None of that shows up in a DCF, but it shapes headlines, protests, and occasionally deal risk.

The Criticism

"A war-tool company"

Gotham has been used in military targeting, border enforcement (including ICE), and predictive-policing contexts. Critics — including some current and former employees — argue the technology enables surveillance and lethal decisions with insufficient public accountability.

The Criticism

Optics risk, not just political risk

Periodic employee walkouts, campus protests, and activist campaigns targeting Palantir's government work create recurring negative press cycles, even in quarters when they don't move revenue at all.

The Response

"We work with the West, by design"

CEO Alex Karp has been unusually blunt in defending the government business, arguing democracies — not authoritarian rivals — should have the best software, and that Palantir deliberately won't sell to adversarial regimes.

The Response

Human-in-the-loop, by policy

Palantir's public position is that its software assists human decision-makers rather than acting autonomously, and that it maintains use-policy and audit commitments around lethal and surveillance applications.

None of this moves the revenue line directly — but it's a real factor in how some institutional allocators, and some prospective employees, decide whether they'll touch the stock or the company at all.
