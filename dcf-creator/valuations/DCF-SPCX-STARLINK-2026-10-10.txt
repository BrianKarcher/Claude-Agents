WHAT THIS FILE IS (READ FIRST)
-------------------------------
This is NOT a standard DCF of SPCX stock. Per the request, it values ONLY the
Connectivity/Starlink segment of SpaceX, treated as if it were an INDEPENDENT
standalone company — not a division that gets its rockets for free from an
affiliated launch provider. Two modeling instructions drive everything below:

  1. "Treat it as an independent segment... it needs to find its launches into
     space." SpaceX's own S-1 discloses that Starlink currently pays NOTHING
     (no inter-segment revenue) for the Falcon 9/Starship launches that deploy
     its satellites — those costs are simply capitalized into Starlink's own
     PP&E at SpaceX's INTERNAL cost. An independent Starlink would instead have
     to buy launch services at an ARM'S-LENGTH MARKET RATE (from SpaceX's Space
     division behaving as an unaffiliated counterparty, or from a competing
     launch provider). Repricing this one item is the single biggest swing
     factor in the entire model — see Section 4 and the "independence discount"
     in Section 7B.
  2. "Assume Starship and Starlink V3 are successful in their intended paths."
     This REMOVES the single largest risk factor SpaceX itself flags for this
     segment (the S-1's Risk Factors devote more space to Starship execution
     risk than to any other single topic). All three scenarios below assume
     Starship achieves commercial cadence and reusability and V3 satellites
     deploy as planned — Bear/Base/Bull differ on subscriber growth, ARPU,
     competitive intensity, and HOW FAST/HOW CHEAP that success translates into
     lower cost per unit of deployed capacity, not on whether Starship works at
     all. If that assumption is wrong, treat even this file's Bear case as
     optimistic (see Section 9, Risk #1).

Because there is no standalone Starlink balance sheet, share count, or market
price, this file's headline outputs are ENTERPRISE VALUE and EQUITY VALUE in
dollars, not a per-share figure. Section 7B and the Disclosures translate these
into context against SPCX's own ~$2.1-2.2T market cap and against external
analyst sum-of-the-parts commentary.

SECTION 1 — BUSINESS SNAPSHOT: WHAT'S BEING VALUED
================================================================================

Starlink is SpaceX's Connectivity segment: broadband + mobile (direct-to-cell)
satellite internet for consumers, enterprises, and governments (incl. the
Starshield national-security product), delivered over a LEO constellation of
~9,600 satellites (as of March 2026) reaching 164 countries/territories. It is,
by a wide margin, the most profitable part of SpaceX today — Connectivity
generated $4,423M of FY2025 operating income while the Space segment LOST
$657M (Starship R&D) and the AI segment LOST $6,355M (xAI/Grok buildout). In a
real sense, Starlink currently subsidizes the rest of SpaceX, not the reverse.

What makes Starlink unusual as a "segment to spin off" is that it is also the
single biggest CUSTOMER of SpaceX's own launch business — 122 of the Falcon
fleet's ~165-170 launches in 2025 were Starlink-dedicated, consuming the
majority of SpaceX's total launch capacity. Under consolidated accounting, this
is invisible: no inter-segment revenue changes hands, and the "cost" that shows
up in Connectivity's books is Space's own build/launch/overhead cost, not a
market price. Independence removes that transfer-pricing subsidy. This file
quantifies exactly how much that subsidy is worth.

Two business lines make up Connectivity revenue:
  - CONSUMER broadband (+ Starlink Mobile/direct-to-cell): subscription + kit
    sales to individuals; ARPU-driven, largest subscriber base, lowest and
    still-declining average revenue per user as international/lower-income
    markets scale.
  - ENTERPRISE & GOVERNMENT: fixed-site enterprise (maritime, aviation, energy,
    retail), carrier MNO deals for direct-to-cell, and government/Starshield —
    smaller base today (42% of Q2 2026 revenue) but growing far faster (+108%
    YoY in Q2 2026 vs. consumer's ~flat-to-declining ARPU-adjusted growth).

The segment's forward story is almost entirely about STARSHIP: V3 satellites
(1 Tbps downlink each, ~8x a V1/V2 Mini satellite's capacity) are too large for
Falcon 9 and can only fly on Starship, which is designed to carry 60 of them per
launch — a 20x jump in deployed capacity per launch versus today's Falcon 9
missions. That capacity leap, not subscriber count alone, is what the Bull case
is really betting on.

SECTION 3 — STRATEGIC CONTEXT: THE TRANSFER-PRICING GAP AND THE STARSHIP BET
================================================================================

3A. WHY CONNECTIVITY'S REPORTED MARGINS OVERSTATE STANDALONE PROFITABILITY

SpaceX's S-1 says it plainly: "For launches of our Starlink satellites, the
Company does not recognize any inter-segment revenue, rather those launch costs
are capitalized in satellites in Property, plant, and equipment, net." The cost
that flows into Connectivity's depreciation is SpaceX's own internal cost to
fly a Falcon 9 — reported externally at roughly $15M for a reused booster's
marginal cost. The price an UNAFFILIATED customer pays SpaceX for that same
launch is roughly $74M (2026 commercial list price) — nearly 5x higher. An
independent Starlink, lacking SpaceX's internal cost structure and insider
pricing, would face something closer to the $74M figure (or a competitor's
equivalent), not the $15M figure, to secure the same launch cadence.

For FY2025 alone: 122 Starlink-dedicated launches at the $74M market rate would
cost ~$9.0B — equal to ~79% of Connectivity's entire $11.4B revenue for the
year, and more than double its reported total capex of $4.2B. This is the starkest
single number in this file: Starlink's reported 38.8% FY2025 operating margin
is, in significant part, a byproduct of being inside SpaceX, not a standalone
economic reality.

3B. THE STARSHIP/V3 OFFSET — WHY "SUCCESS" MATTERS SO MUCH

The reason this doesn't simply make independent Starlink permanently
uneconomic is Starship's capacity leap. A Starship launch costs more in
absolute dollars than a Falcon 9 launch, but is designed to deploy 60 V3
satellites (vs. ~23 V1/V2 Mini satellites on a Falcon 9) delivering a 20x jump
in capacity per launch. Even at a MUCH higher per-launch dollar cost than
Falcon 9, the cost PER UNIT OF DEPLOYED CAPACITY falls sharply once Starship is
flying commercially — this is the entire reason the user's instruction to
"assume Starship and V3 succeed" matters so much to this valuation: it is the
only mechanism by which an independent Starlink's launch-cost burden shrinks
over time rather than growing in line with the fleet forever. Per the S-1,
Starship's own long-run aspirational marginal cost (100+ reuses) is as low as
$2M/launch — this file does NOT assume SpaceX (or a competitor) ever charges an
arm's-length Starlink customer anywhere near that insider figure; see Section 4
for the declining-but-still-well-above-$2M market-rate curve actually used.

3C. COMPETITION

Amazon's Kuiper/Leo constellation (~241 satellites as of early 2026, targeting
~1,600 by mid-2026 under an FCC deadline it has already asked to extend to
2028) and Eutelsat OneWeb (600+ satellites, enterprise/government/aviation
focus, revenue +60% H1 2026) are both real, but both are still a rounding
error next to Starlink's ~9,600-satellite fleet and ~75% share of all active
maneuverable satellites in orbit. Competition is modeled here primarily through
ARPU and Enterprise & Government growth-rate assumptions (Section 4), not
through subscriber-share loss directly.

SECTION 4 — DCF ASSUMPTIONS

MODELING APPROACH
- 10-year explicit forecast: FY2026E-FY2035E, off an FY2026E anchor built from
  two actual quarters (Q1: $3,257M, Q2: $4,291M; H1 actual $7,548M, split
  Consumer $4,371M / Enterprise & Government $3,177M) plus an H2 2026 estimate.
- Unlevered FCF = NOPAT + D&A - CapEx; SBC-adjusted FCF further subtracts an
  allocated stock-based-compensation charge and is what's actually discounted,
  consistent with this folder's convention.
- NOPAT = Operating income x (1 - 15%) when operating income is positive;
  = operating income unchanged in loss years (no assumed tax benefit) —
  this folder's standard convention. (It does not actually bind here:
  operating income stays positive in every year of every scenario; the
  early-year FCF deficits come from capex, not operating losses.)
- CapEx and D&A are modeled from a GROUND-UP satellite/launch build (not a
  generic %-of-revenue plug) because that build is the entire point of this
  exercise — see "THE LAUNCH-COST REPRICING MECHANIC" below.
- Each scenario uses its own WACC, terminal growth rate, and exit multiple.

THE LAUNCH-COST REPRICING MECHANIC (the heart of this file)

Step 1 — satellites launched per year: set directly per scenario from the
2023-2025 history (1,449 / 2,047 / 2,806 implied satellites launched), then
DECELERATING toward a plateau as Starship/V3 ramps — because each V3 satellite
carries ~8x a V1/V2 Mini satellite's capacity, Starlink does NOT need to keep
growing raw satellite COUNT in line with revenue once Starship is flying;
capability per satellite does the work instead. This is the direct numerical
expression of "assume Starship/V3 succeed":

  Satellites launched/yr   2026  2027  2028  2029  2030  2031  2032  2033  2034  2035
  BULL                     3,150 3,680 4,150 4,550 4,880 5,150 5,370 5,540 5,670 5,770
  BASE                     3,050 3,450 3,780 4,030 4,220 4,360 4,460 4,530 4,580 4,615
  BEAR                     2,950 3,250 3,480 3,650 3,770 3,850 3,900 3,930 3,945 3,955

Step 2 — Starship/V3 share of those satellites (rest fly on Falcon 9/V2 Mini,
23 sats/launch; Starship carries 60 sats/launch):
  2026: 5-8% -> 2030: 70-88% -> 2035: 85-97% (Bear/Base/Bull respectively)

Step 3 — cost per launch:
  - Falcon 9: flat at the $74M MARKET (not insider $15M) rate throughout, all
    scenarios — this is the independence adjustment itself.
  - Starship: starts at the Falcon-9-equivalent $74M (2026, pre-commercial)
    and DECLINES as reusability/cadence scale, landing at:
      BULL: $14M by 2035   BASE: $32M by 2035   BEAR: $55M by 2035
    None of these reach SpaceX's own $2M aspirational insider figure even in
    the Bull case by 2035 — a deliberately conservative choice (see Section 9,
    Risk #4): an independent Starlink buying launch as a service should not be
    assumed to receive insider-grade pricing even after Starship matures.

Step 4 — satellite manufacturing cost ($M/satellite, blended, rising as the
fleet mix shifts toward larger V3 satellites): $0.75M (2026, mostly V1/V2
Mini) -> ~$1.0-1.2M by the early 2030s across scenarios (V3's disclosed
~$1.2M/unit cost, partly offset by manufacturing learning-curve gains in Bull).

Step 5 — total "Satellite + Launch" CapEx = (Falcon launches x $74M) +
(Starship launches x scenario launch-cost curve) + (satellites launched x
blended mfg cost), PLUS other Connectivity capex (ground infrastructure, user
terminals) held at 4.5% of revenue. All of it is capitalized and depreciated
straight-line over a 5-year satellite design life (consistent with the ~20%/
year fleet-replacement rate implied by that life), matching how the satellite
industry (Iridium, Viasat, Eutelsat) actually accounts for launch costs. New
capex takes a half-year of depreciation in its first and last years. The
existing fleet ($13,788M net satellite PP&E at 6/30/2026) is depreciated
straight-line over an assumed 2.5-year blended remaining life ($5,515M in
2026 and 2027, $2,757M in 2028, fully written off thereafter).

Step 6 — STRIPPING THE LEGACY D&A OUT OF THE "AS RUN" MARGIN. The "before"
operating margin above already has Connectivity's CURRENT depreciation (on
insider-cost satellites/launches, ground stations, terminals) deducted. Since
Step 5 rebuilds ALL Connectivity D&A from scratch at market-rate launch
pricing, that embedded legacy D&A must be added back first or it is counted
twice. Its size is derived from disclosed figures:
                                     FY2024    FY2025    H1 2026
  Adjusted EBITDA - Operating income  1,843     2,745     1,840
  as % of segment revenue             24.3%     24.1%     24.4%
That ~24% gap includes not just D&A but also the add-backs inside "Adjusted"
EBITDA — principally stock-based compensation, which this model allocates at
~6% of revenue (see below). Netting that out: legacy D&A embedded in the
"as run" margin = 18% of revenue, applied in every year and every scenario.
  Standalone operating income = "as run" OpInc + 18% x revenue - new D&A
JUDGMENT CALL, FLAGGED: 18% is a derived, not a disclosed, figure (SpaceX
does not break out Connectivity D&A). Using 15% instead (a deliberate haircut)
would lower Base EV by ~$20B; using the full 24% gap would raise it further.
CONSEQUENCE WORTH NOTING: because market-rate capex plateaus at ~$12-14B/yr
while revenue keeps compounding, new D&A falls from ~40% of revenue (2026) to
~15% (2035) — below the 18% legacy intensity. Standalone 2035 operating
margins therefore END UP ABOVE the 42% "as run" ceiling in Base (45.0%) and
Bull (50.6%). That is the mechanical result of "Starship succeeds and capacity
per satellite does the work"; it is the most aggressive output of this model
and the first thing a skeptic should push on (Section 9, Risk #10).

STOCK-BASED COMPENSATION: consolidated SBC was $1,470M for 6M 2026 (not
disclosed by segment). Allocated to Connectivity via a revenue-share proxy:
6.2% of Connectivity revenue in 2026, declining to 3.5% by 2035 as the business
matures — flagged explicitly as an estimate, not a disclosed figure.

DISCOUNT RATE DERIVATION: there is no clean public comp for "standalone LEO
satellite broadband carved out of a larger launch company." The closest
observable reference points are satellite-communications incumbents: Viasat's
WACC is ~8.8% (beta ~1.78, cost of debt ~5.1%) — a slower-growing, more mature,
more levered satellite operator than Starlink would be. I set Base WACC (10.5%)
above Viasat's to reflect Starlink's much higher growth-dependent valuation and
real execution risk around the Starship-cost-decline assumption baked into
every scenario here (even though Starship's EXISTENCE is assumed successful,
HOW FAST its cost-per-unit-of-capacity falls is still a live variable this
WACC must price), while still recognizing Starlink's dominant market position
(75% of active satellites in orbit) and now-demonstrated profitability. Bull/
Bear move 100bps in each direction from Base, consistent with this folder's
convention elsewhere.

PROBABILITY WEIGHTS: Bear 35% / Base 45% / Bull 20% — weighted toward Bear
relative to a "neutral" 30/40/30 split, consistent with this agent's
conservative-resolution rule and reflecting that the single most bullish lever
in this model (how fast Starship's market-rate cost per launch falls) is
inherently less certain than the demand-side (subscriber/ARPU) assumptions,
even with execution risk assumed away per the user's instruction. A reader more
convinced Starship's cost curve will collapse quickly could reasonably shift
10-15 points from Bear into Bull.

Base-case WACC of 10.5% and explicit 2026-2035 FCF path fixed) that would be

  Target EV        Implied perpetuity growth rate     vs. this file's Base TGR (3.0%)
  $180,000M (25x EBITDA commentary)       1.89%              -111bps
  $250,000M (35x EBITDA commentary)       4.84%              +184bps
  $350,000M                               6.71%              +371bps
  $500,000M (Morgan Stanley's figure)     7.96%              +496bps

(Perpetuity-growth method only. At Base inputs that method alone gives
$199.9B — Table A — so these growth rates are measured against that figure,
not against the $282.6B blended headline.)

A perpetual growth rate above ~4-5% is difficult to defend for a mature
business discounted at 10.5% (it implies the segment outgrows global nominal
GDP forever). So on a pure perpetuity basis, the low end of the analyst range
($180B) is comfortably supported — it needs LESS growth than this file
assumes — while $250B is at the edge and $350B+ is not reachable without an
implausible terminal assumption.

Through the EXIT-MULTIPLE method (Table B), $250-350B is available at ~11-16x
terminal EBITDA at Base WACC, and $500B needs roughly 24-25x at 10.5% WACC or
~20x at 8.5%. Via the blended method this file actually uses, $500B sits
between Base ($282.6B) and Bull ($670.9B).

Plain conclusion: even after pricing every launch at arm's-length market
rates, the 25-35x EBITDA commentary ($180-250B) looks REASONABLE-TO-
CONSERVATIVE, not stretched. Morgan Stanley's $500B is not unreasonable, but it
requires something close to Bull-case execution (faster Starship cost
decline, 20M subscribers by YE2026, ~50% terminal operating margins) or a
rich terminal multiple — it is not a central-case number on this model.

SECTION 9 — KEY RISKS (SPECIFIC TO THIS ANALYSIS)
================================================================================

1. STARSHIP/V3 EXECUTION RISK IS ASSUMED AWAY, NOT ELIMINATED (VERY HIGH if
   wrong). Per the user's instruction, every scenario here assumes Starship
   achieves commercial cadence and reusability and V3 satellites deploy on
   schedule. SpaceX's own S-1 devotes more Risk Factor space to this than to
   any other topic, explicitly warning that failure would mean "higher
   per-launch costs, slower deployment timelines... and increased overall
   capital requirements." If Starship does NOT succeed as assumed, Starlink
   would be stuck paying Falcon-9-market-rate ($74M) or worse indefinitely,
   with satellite count needing to grow in line with revenue (no capacity-
   per-satellite escape valve) — this file's Bear case would then be
   optimistic, not conservative, and the whole capex-intensity-declines-over-
   time mechanic in Section 4 would reverse.
2. THE MARKET-RATE LAUNCH PRICE ITSELF IS A JUDGMENT CALL (HIGH, and the single
   most consequential one in this file). $74M is SpaceX's own PUBLISHED list
   price to unaffiliated commercial customers; a real independent Starlink
   might negotiate a long-term bulk-volume discount below list (it would be
   SpaceX's/a competitor's single largest customer by volume), or might face
   an even higher price if SpaceX's Space division extracted monopoly rents
   from a known-captive former affiliate. Section 7B's $32.8B "independence
   discount" is the quantified sensitivity to this single assumption.
3. NO STANDALONE BALANCE SHEET EXISTS. The $6.0B net debt allocation (Section
   4) is derived, not disclosed — a $0 (fully unlevered) alternative would add
   exactly $6.0B to every scenario's equity value.
4. STARSHIP COST CURVE DELIBERATELY CAPPED ABOVE THE $2M INSIDER ASPIRATION
   (MEDIUM, conservative by design). Even the Bull case's $14M-by-2035 market
   rate is 7x SpaceX's own stated long-run aspiration — using the full $2M
   aspirational figure instead would raise Bull-case value substantially
   further; not modeled, flagged as the clearest "less-conservative
   alternative" in this file.
5. ENTERPRISE & GOVERNMENT GROWTH DECELERATION IS UNCERTAIN (MEDIUM-HIGH). This
   line has been independently disclosed for only one full quarter (Q2 2026,
   +108% YoY); every deceleration curve in Section 4 is this model's own
   judgment, not a trend fit to multiple data points.
6. SBC AND NET-DEBT SEGMENT ALLOCATIONS ARE PROXIES, NOT DISCLOSED FIGURES
   (MEDIUM). Both use revenue-/PP&E-share allocations of consolidated totals;
   actual segment-specific figures, if SpaceX ever disclosed them, could
   differ meaningfully in either direction.
7. COMPETITION (KUIPER, ONEWEB) COULD PRESSURE ARPU/ENTERPRISE GROWTH FASTER
   THAN MODELED (MEDIUM). Both competitors are explicitly targeting the
   Enterprise & Government mix where Starlink's moat is currently thinnest.
8. NO TRADED PRICE EXISTS TO BENCHMARK AGAINST. Unlike every other file in
   this folder, there is no market price for "standalone Starlink" to compare
   this DCF's output to directly — only SPCX's consolidated price (which
   prices Space and AI too) and secondhand analyst SOTP commentary (Section 8).
9. REGULATORY/SPECTRUM RISK FOR CONTINUED INTERNATIONAL EXPANSION (LOW-MEDIUM,
   a standard risk for any global satcom operator, not unique to the
   independence framing).
10. TERMINAL MARGINS AND CAPEX PLATEAU (HIGH). Base and Bull reach 45.0% and
   50.6% operating margins by 2035 because market-rate capex levels off at
   ~$12-14B/yr while revenue roughly 5-8x's — D&A falls to ~10-15% of revenue
   versus ~18% today. If V3/Starship capacity gains do NOT let satellite
   count plateau as modeled (e.g., demand per subscriber grows faster, or
   spectrum limits force more satellites), capex would keep scaling with
   revenue and these margins would not materialize. Combined with terminal
   value being ~80% of Base EV, this is the assumption a skeptic should test
   first. Separately, the 18% legacy-D&A add-back (Section 4, Step 6) is
   derived, not disclosed: 15% would lower Base EV by ~$20B.

SECTION 10 — INVESTMENT ASSESSMENT / WHAT THIS MEANS
================================================================================

This file is not asking "should you buy SPCX" — it is answering "what is
Starlink actually worth on its own, and what does that say about how SpaceX is
structured." Three conclusions:

1. VERTICAL INTEGRATION IS A REAL, QUANTIFIABLE SUBSIDY, NOT JUST A NARRATIVE.
   Being inside SpaceX is worth ~$33B (~12%) of Base-case value to Starlink
   purely through transfer pricing on launches — before counting any synergy
   from shared manufacturing, talent, or balance-sheet access. This explains,
   in concrete dollar terms, why Musk has never seriously entertained spinning
   Starlink out: its current structure captures value that an independent
   entity could not.
2. THE STARSHIP BET IS DOING MORE WORK THAN THE SUBSCRIBER GROWTH STORY. Every
   scenario here — even Bear — assumes Starship/V3 succeed, and even so, the
   segment is FCF-negative for ~2 years as an independent entity before the
   capacity-per-launch payoff arrives. That near-term capital intensity is
   invisible inside the consolidated SpaceX P&L, and ~80% of Base EV sits in
   the terminal value — i.e., in the post-Starship steady state.
3. THE ANALYST RANGE SURVIVES LAUNCH REPRICING. This file's probability-
   weighted value ($304.5B) sits ABOVE the 25-35x EBITDA commentary
   ($180-250B) and below Morgan Stanley's $500B, even though it is the only
   analysis in that range that explicitly prices launches at arm's length.
   The independence discount is real (~$33B) but modest next to the value
   of the business itself — the bigger swing factors are the terminal
   method (Table A vs. Table B: $200B vs. $365B at Base inputs) and whether
   capex really plateaus once Starship is flying (Section 9, Risk #10).
